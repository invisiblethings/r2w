Preservation rhinoplasty is a newer way of operating on the nose. Instead of extensive removal of bone and cartilage, the surgeon tries to maintain the nose's own structure. It is widely discussed online. The evidence is still young.

This guide explains the idea, what published reviews show, and who it may not suit.

## What is preservation rhinoplasty?

Preservation rhinoplasty is a set of techniques that maintain one or more aspects of the nose's natural structure. A 2024 systematic review describes dorsal, soft tissue and tip preservation, and calls it a more conservative, largely aesthetic approach that avoids extensive resection ([Cureus 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11463265)).

Dorsal preservation applies the idea to the bridge, and the review also covers soft-tissue and tip preservation. Reconstructive rhinoplasty, the comparison in that review, uses techniques such as cartilage grafting. Both aim for a nose that suits your face.

## Who is preservation rhinoplasty suited to?

It suits people seeking cosmetic refinement with limited changes to the bridge. The 2024 review describes the best candidates as having thin skin, minimal dorsal modifications and preserved osseocartilaginous structures, with the closed approach. It describes dorsal preservation as suited to primary cases with a V-shaped hump, standard radix height and uncomplicated axis deviations.

## When is preservation not suitable?

Open or structural approaches are favoured for cases that need extensive dorsal modification, S-shaped nasal bones, complex tip deformities or tip augmentation, according to the same review. It also notes that preservation offers limited scope for major structural change and demands high surgical precision. The review says reconstructive rhinoplasty is generally used for significant structural damage from trauma or medical conditions, often involving grafts. If you have had previous surgery, see [revision rhinoplasty](/procedures/revision-rhinoplasty/).

## What does the evidence say about breathing?

Reviews suggest dorsal preservation does not harm breathing. A systematic review published in the *Journal of Laryngology & Otology* in 2026 covered six studies and 662 patients. Patient-reported obstruction scores improved significantly, objective measures such as acoustic rhinometry and rhinomanometry were maintained or improved, and there was no significant difference from structural techniques ([J Laryngol Otol 2026](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/3052585BF4B37F5AD91E8FD194A0028A)). Obstruction afterwards was rare and usually transient.

The same review states its limits: the studies varied too much to combine, some used only patient-reported outcomes, some lacked blinding, and evidence beyond five years is limited. It reported revision rates below 2% in its abstract and about 4% in one case series in the results, so do not quote either number as a promise.

## What about satisfaction and revisions?

Reported satisfaction is high, but the studies are small. The 2024 Cureus review cites a study in which dorsal preservation improved patient-reported scores at 12 months, and another in which 93.75% of 64 patients reached excellent satisfaction. It also cites 32 open-rhinoplasty patients in whom ligament preservation led to no revisions for tip dropping or malrotation.

Problems have also been reported. In one series of 320 patients, 27 of 44 revision cases had previously undergone dorsal preservation, with hump recurrence and lateral dorsal deviation among the reasons. That does not tell you how likely a revision is after preservation, because the number of preservation patients overall is not given. It does show the technique can fail, and that it can be hard to correct. The review adds that complication profiles and predictability vary between practice settings.

## How reliable is the evidence?

It is limited. The 2024 review says evidence is largely observational, with small samples and case reports, and that long-term data are limited because the technique is relatively new. It did not formally appraise study quality. Be sceptical of marketing that presents preservation as simply "natural", "safer" or "better". Your surgeon's experience with it matters more than the name.

## How is recovery?

Recovery follows the general rhinoplasty pattern. Cleveland Clinic lists a splint for one to two weeks, return to work or school after one to two weeks, swelling and bruising peaking at four to six weeks, about 90% of swelling gone at three months and final results at about a year. We did not find a source showing that preservation shortens that. See recovery day by day.

## What are the risks?

The risks of any rhinoplasty apply. ASPS lists anaesthesia risks, changes in skin sensation, difficulty breathing, infection, septal perforation, poor wound healing or scarring, skin discolouration and swelling, an unsatisfactory appearance and revision surgery. See [risks and complications](/safety/risks-and-complications/).

## What should you ask in Albania?

Ask how many preservation cases the surgeon has done, whether they will use it for your nose, and what they will do if the hump or bridge shifts. Ask for one-year photographs. Preservation is not usually priced separately in published lists we found, so ask for a written quote that states the technique. See [prices in Albania](/cost/prices-2026/).

## What are the main trade-offs in plain terms?

The trade-off is between keeping your own structure and having less room to correct. The 2024 review credits preservation with a conservative approach and good early patient-reported results. It also says the approach offers limited scope for major structural change, needs high surgical precision, and is supported by observational data. The 2026 airway review found function was preserved, but could not combine its studies. In short, preservation may suit a narrow set of noses well, while a surgeon who uses it for every nose is a reason to ask more questions.

If a surgeon recommends it, ask them to explain why your hump, bridge and skin fit the cases the reviews describe, and what the fallback is if the result is not as planned.
