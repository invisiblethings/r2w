Non-surgical rhinoplasty is sometimes called a "liquid nose job". A practitioner injects filler under the skin of the nose to change its outline for a few months. It avoids an operation, but it is not risk-free, and the risks are different from surgery.

This guide explains what it can and cannot do, the serious risks, and who should look elsewhere.

## What is non-surgical rhinoplasty?

Non-surgical rhinoplasty is a treatment that uses dermal fillers to reshape the nose without surgery. Cleveland Clinic says the fillers are temporary and are used to fix small dips, bumps or a droopy tip ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/treatments/11011-rhinoplasty)).

A 2026 narrative review describes the usual method: filler is placed in very small amounts at the radix (the top of the bridge), dorsum and tip, in a deep layer, along the midline, slowly and at low pressure. Practitioners aspirate before injecting, and a blunt cannula is preferred for the bridge. Ultrasound guidance is an emerging aid ([Cureus 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12934192)).

## What can and cannot it do?

It can add volume, and it cannot remove anything. The 2026 review lists mild dorsal depression or radix deficiency, tip droop, minor asymmetry and surface irregularities after rhinoplasty as suitable. It says a severe dorsal hump, deviation or breathing obstruction should be referred for surgery. If you want a smaller nose, filler will make it larger.

Because filler adds volume, a nose can look bigger or broader if too much is used. The 2022 expert paper notes that over-injecting the tip can make it bulbous ([Aesthet Surg J Open Forum 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9337225)).

## Which fillers are used and how long do they last?

Hyaluronic acid is the filler the review recommends for the nose, and it lasts about 6 to 12 months. It can be dissolved with an enzyme, hyaluronidase. The review reports that calcium hydroxylapatite lasts 12 to 18 months and cannot be reversed, poly-L-lactic acid builds gradually for up to two years, and PMMA is long-lasting, irreversible and carries a risk of granuloma. Only hyaluronic acid is recommended. If a practitioner offers a permanent or non-reversible filler for your nose, ask why.

## What are the risks, including vascular occlusion?

The most serious risk is vascular occlusion, where filler blocks or compresses a blood vessel. The 2026 review reports an overall complication rate below 1%. For vascular occlusion it gives 0.35%, visual loss 0.09%, skin necrosis 0.08% and infection 0.07%. Vascular occlusion accounts for about 80% of filler-related ischaemic events. Necrosis most often affects the nasal tip and appears within hours. These percentages come from a narrative review, which the authors say may be biased and not generalisable, so treat them as approximate.

Early warning signs are pain, pale skin and a mottled, net-like colour, which can progress to tissue death or loss of vision. The review says treatment is high-dose hyaluronidase, ideally within 4 hours of symptoms, with urgent referral to an eye specialist for any vision change, within about 90 minutes. It reports about 84% partial or full recovery of vascular occlusion cases, especially when treated within one to five days.

Milder effects are common: redness, swelling, tenderness and bruising, usually settling within 7 to 10 days. Bluish discolouration (the Tyndall effect) can occur if filler is placed too superficially. Delayed reactions occur in under 0.5% of cases.

## Why does the nose carry extra risk?

Filler in the nose is placed close to important arteries. The 2022 expert paper identifies the columellar, lateral nasal and lateral dorsal arteries as danger zones, and treats sites lateral to the midline as high risk. It recommends monitoring for at least 15 minutes for signs of ischaemia and having hyaluronidase available for immediate treatment. The 2026 review adds that Doppler ultrasound can map vessels, especially in revision cases, and that injections should stop at blanching or disproportionate pain.

## What should you check before agreeing?

Ask who is injecting, what training they have, which filler they use and whether hyaluronidase is on hand. The review recommends hyaluronic acid, injection in the midline in small amounts, and screening for vascular, previous-filler and psychological history. Ask where to go if something goes wrong at night or after you fly home. If you notice severe pain, pale or mottled skin, or any change in vision after injection, treat it as an emergency: call the local emergency number (127 in Albania, 112 in the EU) and say you had nose filler.

## Who should not have it?

The 2026 review lists several groups: people with a severe hump or deviation or breathing obstruction, pregnancy or breastfeeding, active infection, autoimmune disease or bleeding disorders, recent nasal surgery or trauma, prior silicone or unknown permanent filler, and people on anticoagulants or corticosteroids unless this is managed. It also says body dysmorphic disorder, a condition in which a person is preoccupied with perceived flaws in their appearance, must be screened for. If you feel distress about your nose that seems out of proportion, talk to your doctor before any treatment, surgical or not.

## How does it compare with surgery?

Non-surgical rhinoplasty has lower morbidity, faster recovery and reversibility with hyaluronic acid, but less long-term structural refinement than surgery. The review reports satisfaction of 80 to 100% soon after treatment, and above 80% at 12 months, but it is a narrative review, so it may include selection bias. Repeated filler every year costs money too. For a permanent change, see [septorhinoplasty](/procedures/septorhinoplasty/) or [open rhinoplasty](/procedures/open-rhinoplasty/).
