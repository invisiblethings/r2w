A droopy nasal tip is a tip that points downward, sometimes making the nose look longer. For some people it drops further when they smile. The American Society of Plastic Surgeons (ASPS) and Cleveland Clinic both list a "drooping" tip among the concerns rhinoplasty can address.

A droopy tip can be natural, or it can follow earlier surgery. This guide explains what causes it, how it is measured, which techniques lift it, and what the evidence honestly shows.

## What causes a droopy tip?

A tip droops because of its support, its shape, and in some people the muscles of the upper lip. A paper on correcting drooping tips (PMC) lists:

- **Static factors:** a malpositioned or misshapen septum, upper and lower lateral cartilages, and the ligaments between them.
- **Dynamic factors:** the depressor septi nasi muscle and the levator labii superioris alaeque nasi muscle, which pull the tip down when you smile.
- **Scar contracture:** scarring over time can pull a newly positioned tip downward.

After rhinoplasty, StatPearls says tip malrotation can come from insufficient tip support, which causes ptosis (drooping), or from overly aggressive suturing or cephalic trimming, which causes the opposite problem, over-rotation.

## How do surgeons assess a droopy tip?

A surgeon assesses a droopy tip with photographs and angle measurements, then looks at what holds the tip up. The PMC paper defined a drooping tip as a nose-to-lip (nasolabial) angle below about 90 to 95 degrees in men or 100 to 105 degrees in women. It also measured tip rotation, the angle between columella and face, and projection using the Goode ratio (an ideal of about 0.55 to 0.60).

StatPearls describes the usual photographs (front, sides, three-quarter, below, above) and gives a nasolabial angle of about 95 to 110 degrees in women and 90 to 95 degrees in men. Normal ranges differ between sources, and between faces, so your surgeon should explain what they aim for on your nose.

Ask the surgeon to show you a smiling photo too. If the tip drops on smiling, tell them.

## Which technique usually lifts a droopy tip?

A droopy tip is usually treated with [tip rhinoplasty](/procedures/tip-rhinoplasty/) or full [septorhinoplasty](/procedures/septorhinoplasty/). In the 41-patient series in PMC, the authors used closed or open septorhinoplasty according to need, and combined several steps:

| Step | How often (of 41) |
|---|---|
| Straightening and shortening the septum | 41 |
| Tongue-in-groove fixation (fixing the tip cartilages to the septum in a new position) | 36 (87.8%) |
| Cephalic resection of the lateral cartilages | 29 (70.7%) |
| Re-orienting sutures | 18 (43.9%) |
| Columellar strut | 8 (19.5%) |
| Excision of the depressor septi muscle | 4 |

Cephalic trimming is a common rotation tool. StatPearls says to leave 7 to 8 mm of lateral cartilage, and warns that removing an additional wedge raises upward rotation at the cost of stability. Trimming too much causes alar retraction, so the nostril rim lifts too far.

Notice that in this series the cartilage strut was a minor part. Surgeons differ on which supports they prefer, so the right question for your surgeon is why they chose their plan for you.

If your tip is also wide, see [bulbous tip](/nose-concerns/bulbous-tip/). StatPearls says the open approach is preferred for tip work. See [open rhinoplasty](/procedures/open-rhinoplasty/).

## What result is realistic?

A realistic result is a tip that sits higher and a nose-to-lip angle that moves toward the normal range, not a perfectly rotated tip. In the PMC series of 41 patients followed for 36 months, the nasolabial angle rose from an average of 77.3 to 93.9 degrees. Tip rotation improved. Tip projection did not change significantly. There were no major or late complications, and two cases of minor infection around the columellar stitches resolved.

The same paper says the post-operative angles were often "ideal or improved" rather than fully normal, and describes treatment of drooping tips as controversial. Its limitations are plain: it is retrospective, small, without a control group, measured from photographs by two examiners, and some numbers are inconsistent within the paper. Treat it as an illustration, not a promise.

The tip also heals slowly. MedlinePlus says the tip may remain swollen and numb for several months, and Cleveland Clinic says full results usually appear after a year. See [swelling month by month](/recovery/swelling-month-by-month/).

## What can go wrong?

Tip lifting carries the general risks of rhinoplasty. Read the risks and complications guide. Specific to the tip, StatPearls warns of alar retraction after aggressive cephalic trimming, and of breathing obstruction if the internal valve or nostril wall is weakened. A paper on septorhinoplasty complications recommends alar rim grafts for alar retraction. Revision is possible: see revision rhinoplasty.

A non-surgical option exists. Cleveland Clinic lists a droopy tip among the concerns filler may address, and the effect is temporary. See non-surgical rhinoplasty.

## What to ask the surgeon

Ask what is causing your droop: support, cartilage shape or muscle. Ask how the surgeon will hold the tip in its new position, and how they measure the result. Ask for photos of patients with a similar angle, including smiling photos. See questions to ask a clinic and how to verify a surgeon.

## How should you prepare for the consultation?

Bring photos of yourself smiling and in profile, and say what bothers you: the angle, the length of the nose, or the way the tip drops when you talk. The studies cited here measured the angle between nose and lip on standardised photographs, so ask your surgeon how they will measure it before and after. Tell them about any earlier nose surgery, because a tip that droops after surgery needs a different plan from one that has always drooped. StatPearls lists prior nasal surgery among the history points a surgeon should ask about. Ask for the plan in writing, including which supports will be used for the tip.
