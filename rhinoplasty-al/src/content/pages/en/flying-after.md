The range given by the professional and public-health sources we reviewed is 7 to 10 days after facial surgery, which includes rhinoplasty. The operating surgeon, who knows how your nose is healing, decides when you can fly. This guide explains where the range comes from, why it exists, and how to plan.

## How soon can you fly after rhinoplasty?

The guidance is to wait 7 to 10 days. The American Society of Plastic Surgeons (ASPS) says that after facial procedures, "including facelifts, eyelid surgery, rhinoplasty, and laser treatments", patients should wait 7 to 10 days before flying. The NHS gives the same range for facial procedures and tummy tucks, and 5 to 7 days after breast surgery or liposuction.

We did not find a peer-reviewed study that measured the safest day to fly after rhinoplasty, so this is guidance from professional and public health bodies, not a trial result. Treat any single number as a minimum, not a promise.

## Why is there a waiting period?

There are two reasons: blood clots and healing. The NHS says air travel and major surgery raise the risk of a potentially life-threatening blood clot. ASPS says that flights and surgery each carry clot risk, and combining them raises the chance of blood clots and pulmonary embolism, which can be fatal.

The NHS page on deep vein thrombosis lists risk factors including recent surgery and journeys of more than four hours by plane, car or train. Dehydration and being unable to move around are also listed.

The second reason is the nose itself. In the first week the nose is at its most swollen and the splint and packing are in place. ASPS says pain and swelling are most pronounced in the first week. StatPearls says splints and tape are usually removed at 7 days, ASPS says about one week, and Cleveland Clinic and MedlinePlus say one to two weeks. A flight before the splint is off is a decision to discuss with the surgeon.

## Who decides?

The surgeon who operated on you decides. The range above is general. Your own date depends on:

- what was done (a simple tip operation, a [septorhinoplasty](/procedures/septorhinoplasty/), or a [revision](/procedures/revision-rhinoplasty/));
- whether packing or a splint is still in place;
- whether you had bleeding or other complications;
- your own risk of clots, which the NHS links to factors such as smoking, previous clots, hormonal medicines, obesity and cancer.

Ask the surgeon at your first consultation how many days they want to see you after surgery, and whether they will see you again before you leave. Write the answer down. Do not rely on a coordinator or travel agent for medical clearance.

## How should you plan the trip?

Plan to stay longer than the minimum, and keep your flight flexible. The NHS says that holidaying straight after surgery is "unrealistic" and can increase the risk of things going wrong. It lists resting, and avoiding alcohol, sunbathing, sightseeing and swimming. Read [how long to stay in Albania](/recovery/how-long-to-stay-in-albania/) and [planning your trip](/travel/planning-your-trip/).

Practical points that follow from the sources:

- Choose a flight date you can move. If you book exactly 7 days after surgery and the surgeon says 10, you pay to change it or feel pressure to fly.
- Ask whether the splint will be off before you fly. Ask what to tell airline staff and airport security about it.
- On the journey, follow the surgeon's advice on moving around and drinking water. The NHS lists dehydration and immobility as risk factors for clots.
- Take your medicines and the surgeon's contact details in your hand luggage.

## What should you watch for after you fly?

Watch for signs of a clot, and for any bleeding or breathing problem. The NHS lists the symptoms of deep vein thrombosis: throbbing pain in one leg (usually the calf or thigh), swelling in one leg, red, blue or darkened skin around the painful area, and swollen veins. If you have these and also feel short of breath or have chest pain, the NHS says to call emergency services or go to the emergency department, not to drive yourself.

For nose-specific warning signs, read [problems after you get home](/safety/problems-after-you-get-home/).

## What if you must fly sooner?

Speak to the operating surgeon before you decide. Do not decide by yourself. A family emergency or a work obligation is a reason to call the surgeon, who can tell you whether it is safe for your nose and your risk of clots, or whether to wait.

## What should you ask before you book the return flight?

Ask three questions at your consultation and write down the answers. First, on which day after surgery will you check my nose and remove the splint? Second, what is the earliest day you would clear me to fly, and what would delay it? Third, who do I call if I have a problem at the airport or after I land? The NHS advises asking what happens if complications occur and where follow-up appointments will take place. If a clinic coordinator tells you that you can fly on the day after surgery, ask the surgeon to confirm it in writing, because the guidance from ASPS and the NHS is 7 to 10 days.
